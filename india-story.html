International Integration • LSI Logic • 2005–2008
Going Global
How a storage engineering organization broke through chronic resource constraints by building its first international partnerships — and learned to work across cultures in the process.
LSI Logic — Storage Division — Colorado
The Situation
An Organization Stretched Thin
LSI's storage division was perennially under-resourced. Technical debt climbed. Projects queued faster than teams could deliver. The firmware codebase was closely guarded — but the products had a significant software layer that was equally critical and deeply understaffed.

The Opening
The Software Layer Was the Way In
Engineering management was reluctant to open the firmware to outside contributors. But the software component — equally starved for resources — offered a natural entry point. The proposal: find engineering partners in India who could take on the software work and begin proving the model.
A strategic reframe — meeting the organization where it was willing to move.
The Network
Executive Access Through Trusted Connections
A strategic consultant — an executive who had built US West's cellular business relationships across India — opened doors that would have taken months to find. Through her C-suite network, executive-level meetings were secured at five of India's most prominent IT services firms.

On The Ground
Two Weeks. Two Cities. Five Firms.
A four-person evaluation team spent two weeks across Chennai and Bengaluru — conducting executive-level interviews, assessing technical depth and cultural alignment, and building a structured selection process that the organization could stand behind.

The Scale
30+ Engineers Integrated
Within months, over thirty contracted engineers were embedded across multiple projects — expanding LSI's software delivery capacity and making a real dent in the backlog that had held the organization back.
Each dot represents an engineer brought into the organization
The Harder Problem
Building the Bridge
Signing contracts was the easy part. The real work was cultural integration. Some internal teams pushed back on quality — but the root cause wasn't talent overseas. It was requirements that weren't written clearly enough to cross an ocean. The fix was process and communication, not blame.
