The Courage
to Kill a Project

When facts are clear and no one wants to see them
Zahirudeen Premji  •  Director of Program Management  •  LSI Corporation
The Situation

An Acquisition
Left Behind

When I took over as Director of Program Management at LSI, I inherited a project in another state — the remnant of a small acquisition made before my arrival. A team of 3–4 people. A senior program manager assigned to help them. And no meaningful progress to show for it.
On paper, it was a project. In practice, it was a commitment no one wanted to examine too closely.

The Investigation

Everyone Knew.
Nobody Said It.

I started talking to people across the organization — stakeholders, sponsors, peers. What I heard was consistent: everyone sensed this project was failing. But the conversation never happened.

The Turning Point

Factual. Blunt.
To the Point.

After a thorough review, my conclusion was unambiguous: this project had no viable path to value. Resources were being consumed. Talent was being wasted. The only responsible course was termination — and it needed to happen at the right level.

The Approach

I arranged an executive-level review at the Portfolio Management Committee — composed of the company's senior executives — and laid out the case with facts, not politics. No hedging. No softening. No contrary arguments were possible because none existed.

The Presentation

The Room
Goes Quiet

The Portfolio Management Committee heard the full case. Every fact was on the table. The project's lack of progress. The resource drain. The absence of any credible path forward. The recommendation was clear: terminate the project and redeploy the resources.
What surprised me was how few objections there were. When the facts are laid out with clarity and courage, the room often already knows the answer.

Decision Made.
One Week Later.

Within seven days of the PMC presentation, the project was formally terminated. The team was reassigned to work that mattered. The resources that had been quietly disappearing into a stalled initiative were redirected to initiatives with measurable outcomes.

The Leadership Principle

Organizations don't fail
because they start bad projects.
They fail because they
can't stop them.

Being factual, blunt, and to the point is not unkind. It is the clearest form of respect — for the team's time, the organization's resources, and the potential locked up in every initiative you free people to work on instead.
